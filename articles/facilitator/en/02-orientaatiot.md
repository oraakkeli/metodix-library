# Orientations N / S / R / E / D

The orientation states **from which perspective the future is studied**. It is the study's most important starting choice: it shapes thesis wording, panel composition, and how you act between rounds. The same topic yields a different study under a different orientation — so make the choice deliberately, not by default. In Pronoia the orientation also steers the PIRE inter-round assistant.

## Five orientations — and when to choose which

**N — Normative.** Desirability and the desired state. "What kind of future is good?" Leans toward consensus: seeking a shared view of the goal. **Choose when** the question is about will and values: vision, policy, goals.

**S — Strategic.** Feasibility and agency. "How is the goal reached, who acts?" Emphasizes means and options. **Choose when** you are looking for means and mechanisms: implementation, steering, resources.

**R — Radical.** Ruptures and protecting deviance. "What could change fundamentally? Which assumptions break?" Protects minority views — convergence is not forced, because the deviant signal may be the most important. **Choose when** you want to challenge the taken-for-granted: disruptions, wild cards.

**E — Emergent / abductive.** New, still-forming phenomena. An observational stance: what is arising without anyone's plan? **Choose when** the priority is to detect bottom-up change — the panel is composed of observers (what they see, not what they know).

**D — Intentional combination (N+S+R).** A mixed mode where different questions may follow different logics. The default orientation for multifaceted studies. **Choose when** the topic carries value, means and rupture questions at once — one panel, orientations differentiated at thesis level. On the planning side there is also **D+** (four-path): D and E in parallel with two separate panels — requires more resources.

## Rule of thumb

- Do you want to know what **should** be done? Values → N, implementation → S.
- What if everything changes? → R.
- What is **already** happening in the field? → E.
- All the intentional ones together? → D. Also what no one is planning? → D+.

## Same topic, different studies

Example: "AI in higher education 2035". N asks what role for AI in teaching **would be good**. S asks **how** the desired role is achieved and who acts. R asks **what if** the meaning of degrees breaks entirely. E gathers observers to report **what is already happening** in the everyday life of students and teachers. The topic is the same — the study, the panel and the theses are different.

## Why it matters

The orientation is not a mere label but **constrains methodological choices**. For example, under R there is no convergent "central value only" feedback, because it would erase the very deviance you want to protect. Under N, consensus is a natural goal. Pronoia respects this: PIRE derives the inter-round operations from the orientation.

## In practice

The orientation is chosen when the study is created (design). A single thesis's logic can still be fine-tuned with the P/D tag (probability → consensus, desirability → dissensus), which overrides the orientation default for that thesis.

## Facilitator's checklist

- Do not confuse the orientation with the topic — the same topic can be framed in many ways.
- Make the choice deliberately and record the rationale: including what the choice excludes.
- If an assistant or a colleague recommends an orientation, ask for the case for the second-best option too — a good choice comes from comparison.
- When unsure with a multifaceted topic, D is a safe starting point; a purely observational need points to E.
