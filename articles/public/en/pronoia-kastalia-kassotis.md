# Pronoia, Kastalia and Kassotis — Origin Stories
**Pronoia, Kastalia and Kassotis**

Origin stories for the facilitator and the panelists

Delphi Planning Ecosystem (DPE) · Pronoia · Delphi Analysis Engine (DAE)

Metodix Ltd · July 2026

> *Data and interpretation kept apart. At Delphi the god gave the signs, but it was humans who made the meaning --- and Pausanias, whose account these stories draw on, always distinguished what he saw from what he was told. Pronoia carries the same principle forward: the AI carries the data; the human makes the interpretation.*

**A. For the Facilitator: Two Springs and the Temple of Foresight**

On the slopes of Mount Parnassus, at Delphi, asking about the future was a process --- not an event. No one walked straight up to the oracle. When a traveller entered the city, Pausanias tells us, the temples stood in a row, and the fourth of them was the **temple of Athena Pronoia** --- rendered in the English edition simply as **Athena Forethought**. In antiquity the name was read in two ways: Pronaia, "she who stands before the temple", and Pronoia, "foresight". Both readings hold true for this platform as well: **Pronoia is the place you pass through before understanding is born.** It does not predict the future --- it organises the journey on which knowledge about the future is weighed together.

Next to Pronoia\'s temple lay the precinct of the hero Phylacus --- a name that means **the Guardian**. The Delphians told how Phylacus defended the sanctuary against the Persians, and when the Gauls attacked centuries later, the same Guardian appeared once more in its defence. In Pronoia, too, a guardian lives next door to Foresight: anonymity, data protection and research ethics, standing at the gate --- silent but immovable.

Along the way there are two springs, and from them your assistants take their names.

**Kastalia --- the assistant at the threshold**

**Kastalia** rises outside the sanctuary, by the entrance --- Pausanias describes its water as sweet to drink and pleasant to bathe in. Everyone purified themselves there: the questioners, the priesthood and the Pythia herself. Kastalia never prophesied --- she made sure that nothing unclear or impure was carried into the temple. In your work, Kastalia walks beside you through the first two phases:

> **In planning (DPE, Delphi Planning Ecosystem)** Kastalia helps clarify what is being asked and of whom: the phenomenon description, the research orientation, the composition of the panel and the wording of the theses. In the fore-temple at Delphi were inscribed the maxims of the seven sages, one of which read "Nothing in excess" --- Kastalia sees to it that every thesis is sharp enough to withstand the panel\'s argumentation, and that none of them leads the respondent.
>
> **In data collection (Pronoia)** Kastalia moves to the gate to receive the panelists. With each one she holds a preparatory conversation before the actual round --- a modern rite of purification --- and makes sure that everyone enters the dialogue understanding the question and speaking in their own words.

**The Lesche --- the room of dialogue**

And where does the dialogue take place? Inside the sanctuary stood a building called the **Lesche** --- the "place of talk" --- where, according to Pausanias, the Delphians of old gathered to discuss the more serious matters and legendary history, its walls adorned with the great paintings of Polygnotus. The sanctuary of Delphi thus contained, quite literally, a centre for dialogue. **Pronoia\'s panel rounds are its Lesche:** a space where experts, anonymous to one another, weigh arguments in the middle of the sanctuary --- not at the gate, not in the innermost chamber, but in a shared room.

**Kassotis --- the researcher\'s assistant**

**Kassotis** is the second spring --- and it rises inside the sanctuary, by the temple. Pausanias writes that its water sinks under the ground and makes the women seers in the shrine of the god --- and that the spring took its name from a **nymph of Parnassus**. Kassotis, then, is not merely a place but a figure: a being of the mountain itself, who feeds the seeing of things unseen. You meet her only in the third phase:

> **In analysis (DAE, Delphi Analysis Engine)** Kassotis opens the panel\'s speech layer by layer: arguments, dialogue moves, discourses, deep structures, tensions and weak signals --- tirelessly, method after method, quality gate by quality gate. But note the division of labour, inherited directly from Delphi: **Kassotis is the water, not the voice.** She feeds interpretation, but she never utters the oracle. Synthesis, conclusions and the final word belong to you --- just as at Delphi the god gave the signs, but the human made the meaning.

And the finest detail of the story: both springs draw their water from the same groundwater of Parnassus. One body of knowledge, two springs --- one surfaces at the gate in full view of everyone, the other in the innermost chamber for you alone. **Kastalia prepares, Kassotis deepens; you are the one who sees.**

**B. For Panelists: Welcome to Pronoia**

Around 1,850 years ago the Greek travel writer **Pausanias** toured Delphi, home of the most famous oracle in the world, and wrote down everything he saw. Entering the city he saw the temples standing in a row --- and one of them was dedicated to **Athena Pronoia, Athena Forethought**. That is where this platform takes its name: pronoia means foresight. But even in antiquity it was understood that answers about the future did not come ready-made --- they were born through asking, discussion and interpretation. **In Pronoia the future is not predicted; it is reasoned out together.** You have been invited to the panel because your views and your arguments are part of that reasoning.

At the door you are welcomed by **Kastalia**. She takes her name from the spring that rose by the entrance to the sanctuary of Delphi --- every traveller paused at its edge before stepping inside, and Pausanias praised its water as sweet to drink. Kastalia talks with you before the actual round: she opens up the questions and helps you put your thoughts into words and sharpen your reasoning, if you wish. Two things she never does: she does not judge your views, and she never argues on your behalf.

The discussion itself takes place somewhere with a precedent: inside the sanctuary of Delphi stood a building called the **Lesche**, the "place of talk", where people gathered to discuss serious matters. The panel is Pronoia\'s Lesche. There your voice is heard anonymously among the others, so that your argument carries weight by its content, not by who says it. And just as at Delphi the Guardian hero Phylacus lived next to the temple of Foresight and protected the sanctuary, in Pronoia your answers are guarded by anonymity and data protection.

When the rounds are over, your answers do not vanish into a summary table. Deeper inside works a second assistant, **Kassotis** --- named after the nymph of Parnassus whose spring, according to Pausanias, flowed underground into the innermost chamber of the temple and gave rise to seeing. Kassotis helps the researcher see the structures and tensions in the panel\'s arguments --- but interpretations and conclusions are always made by humans. **The AI carries the water; seeing is human work.**

Two words were carved into the wall of the temple at Delphi: Know thyself. The panel exists because a group of knowledgeable people, strangers to one another, sees further together than anyone alone. **Welcome to the spring --- the journey to the temple begins here.**

**Source and Author**

The details of these stories are based on an ancient source: **Pausanias, Periegesis Hellados ("Description of Greece"), Book 10 (Phocis and Delphi)**, written in the 2nd century AD. Key passages: 10.8.6--7 (the temple of Athena Pronoia and the precinct of Phylacus the Guardian), 10.8.9--10 (the Castalian spring), 10.23.2 (Phylacus as defender of the sanctuary), 10.24.1 (the temple maxims "Know thyself" and "Nothing in excess"), 10.24.7 (the spring of Cassotis and the nymph of Parnassus), and 10.25.1 (the Lesche, "place of talk"). Freely readable in English in the translation of W.H.S. Jones and H.A. Ormerod (Loeb Classical Library, 1918), for example in the Theoi and Perseus online libraries.

**Pausanias** was a traveller and geographer of the 2nd century AD, probably from Lydia in Asia Minor, who toured Roman-era Greece and described its sanctuaries, monuments and the legends attached to them in a ten-book work --- the most precise travel guide of antiquity. He carefully distinguished what he saw from what he was told ("it is said that\..."), and archaeology has since confirmed the accuracy of his descriptions: at the excavations of Delphi his text has served as a map.

> ***A methodological kindred spirit:** Pausanias kept data and interpretation apart 1,850 years ago. The same principle carries all three phases of Pronoia --- DPE prepares, Pronoia collects, DAE analyses, and the interpretation is always made by the researcher.*
